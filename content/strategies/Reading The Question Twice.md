## The error

You did everything right and answered a question that was not asked. You found `x` when it
wanted `2x`. You found how many failed when it wanted how many passed. You found the area
when it wanted the perimeter.

This is not carelessness in the ordinary sense. It is a predictable consequence of how
these questions are written: the actual question usually arrives *after* a paragraph of
setup, so by the time you reach it you have already started solving.

## The habit

**Before computing, underline the exact quantity requested. After computing, read that
underline again.**

Two seconds at each end. That is the entire technique.

## What the test does to exploit this

The wrong options are not padding. When a question asks for `2x`, the value of `x` is one
of the five options. When it asks how many did *not* qualify, the number that did is there
too. Your answer appearing in the list is not evidence that you are right — very often it
is evidence that you fell for the trap the question was built around.

## The phrasings that catch people

- **"must be true"** versus **"could be true"**. Completely different questions. Must-be-
  true dies to a single counterexample.
- **"EXCEPT"** and **"NOT"**, usually capitalised, and read straight past anyway.
- **"how many more"** rather than "how many".
- **"approximately"** — a signal that you are allowed to estimate, and often that exact
  computation will be too slow to finish.
- **Units that change mid-question.** Given in minutes, answer in hours. Given a radius,
  options in terms of diameter.
- **"of the following"** in verbal, which restricts you to the options given rather than
  the best answer you can imagine.

## The same failure in verbal

On Reading Comprehension the question stem tells you what *kind* of answer is acceptable —
what the passage says, what it implies, what the author is doing. Answering an inference
question with a true detail is precisely the same mistake as finding `x` when asked for
`2x`.

## What the test actually needs from you

You do not need dictionary definitions. You need three things, in this order:

1. **Charge** — is the word positive, negative, or neutral? This alone eliminates options.
2. **Rough sense** — "something like stubborn" is usually enough to answer correctly.
3. **Precise meaning** — only needed to separate the last two options.

Most study effort goes into (3), while most points are decided by (1) and (2). Learning
200 words to charge-and-rough-sense beats learning 60 words to dictionary precision.

## What to actually learn

**The words this test keeps using.** GRE vocabulary is drawn from academic prose — words
about arguing, doubting, praising, undermining, and hedging. Words like *ostensibly*,
*tenuous*, *equivocate*, *substantiate*, *lauded*, *specious*, *tenable*, *disparate*.

Not obscure nouns. The test cares about the words with which scholars disagree politely.

## Learn words in groups by charge

Isolated words fall out of your head. Words in a charged group stick, and the grouping is
directly usable when you need to eliminate options fast.

- **Praise** — laud, extol, venerate, esteem, exalt
- **Criticise** — censure, deride, disparage, denigrate, excoriate, impugn
- **Fake or doubtful** — spurious, apocryphal, specious, dubious, ersatz
- **Stubborn** — obstinate, intransigent, obdurate, recalcitrant, intractable
- **Brief** — ephemeral, fleeting, transient, evanescent, transitory
- **Wordy** — verbose, prolix, garrulous, loquacious, discursive

Notice that these groups are exactly what
[Sentence Equivalence By Pairs](./Sentence%20Equivalence%20By%20Pairs.md) needs. Studying
by group and answering by pair are the same skill.

## The words that punish you most

The genuinely dangerous ones are not the unfamiliar words. They are the **common words
used in a secondary sense**, because you will not notice you have got them wrong:

- *qualify* — to limit or moderate, not to be eligible
- *arrest* — to stop, as in "arrest the decline"
- *economy* — thrift, restraint, sparseness of means
- *check* — to restrain
- *wanting* — lacking
- *entertain* — to consider, as in "entertain a possibility"
- *appropriate* (verb) — to take for oneself
- *discriminating* — perceptive, able to tell fine distinctions

## How to review

Words you missed enter a spaced schedule automatically. That is the right structure: the
gap before you next see a word grows while you keep getting it right, and collapses when
you do not.

Two habits make it work:

- **Test recall before recognition.** Seeing the word and remembering the meaning is the
  direction the test uses. Reading a definition and nodding is not studying.
- **Keep a sentence, not just a definition.** A word in context survives; a word on a card
  usually does not.

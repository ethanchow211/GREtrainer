## The premise

Every wrong option on this test was written deliberately, to catch a particular reader
making a particular error. None of them are filler. Once you know the catalogue, a wrong
option stops looking like a plausible answer and starts looking like an accusation.

## Quant traps

- **The intermediate value.** You solved for `x` correctly; the question wanted `x + 3`.
  Both are options.
- **The sign flip.** Present whenever the work involves distributing a negative or
  multiplying an inequality through by one.
- **The reciprocal or the complement.** Asked for the fraction that failed, and both `3/4`
  and `1/4` are offered.
- **The right answer to the previous step.** Endemic in multi-step word problems.
- **Order-of-magnitude bait.** Correct digits, decimal point moved.
- **The naive sum.** In percent change, adding percentages instead of multiplying factors.
  A 20% rise followed by a 25% fall is not a 5% fall.

## Verbal traps

- **True but not asked.** Supported by the passage, and an answer to a different question.
  The most common wrong answer in Reading Comprehension.
- **Too extreme.** Academic prose hedges. An option containing "proves", "never", "all",
  or "the sole cause" is usually wrong about a passage that said "suggests".
- **Out of scope.** Reasonable, worldly, sensible — and not in the passage. This test does
  not reward general knowledge.
- **The distortion.** Takes a real claim and strengthens, reverses, or shifts its subject
  slightly. The hardest one, because it is mostly right.
- **Right idea, wrong part of the passage.** Accurate about paragraph 1, asked about
  paragraph 3.
- **The unpaired synonym.** On Sentence Equivalence, a word that fits the sentence
  beautifully and has no partner among the options. See
  [Sentence Equivalence By Pairs](./Sentence%20Equivalence%20By%20Pairs.md).

## How to use this when stuck

Down to two options and no progress? Stop asking "which is right?" and ask **"what error
is each of these designed to catch?"**

If you can name the mistake behind one and cannot name one behind the other, you have your
answer.

> [!tip]
> When you miss a question, the useful thing to record is not the topic. It is which trap
> caught you. Over a few weeks that list gets short and personal, and it is far more
> actionable than "weak at geometry".

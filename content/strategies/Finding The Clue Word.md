## The premise

A well-made Text Completion or Sentence Equivalence question has exactly one defensible
answer. That is only possible if the sentence itself contains enough information to force
it. **Something in the sentence tells you what the blank must mean.** Your job is to find
that thing before the options get a chance to influence you.

## The method

1. **Read the sentence with the blank left empty.**
2. **Find the clue** — the phrase that defines or constrains the blank.
3. **Say your own word out loud**, before reading the options. Even a clumsy one. "Some
   word meaning fake."
4. **Only then read the options**, and find the one closest to your word.

Step 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that does the work. Once you have read
five sophisticated options, several will sound plausible, because your sense of the
sentence has been reshaped by reading them. Committing to your own word first inoculates
you against that.

## The two signal types

**Contrast** — the blank opposes something already stated:

> although, despite, yet, however, nevertheless, whereas, in contrast, far from, rather
> than, notwithstanding, ironically, surprisingly, on the contrary

**Continuation** — the blank restates or extends something already stated:

> because, since, therefore, thus, indeed, moreover, in fact, consequently, and, similarly

A colon or semicolon almost always signals continuation: what follows explains or restates
what came before. That makes punctuation itself a clue.

> [!tip]
> Circle the contrast or continuation word. On a two- or three-blank question, that single
> mark often determines which blank you can solve first — and one solved blank frequently
> forces the others.

## When you do not know the words

You can still make progress. Decide from the clue whether the blank is **positive or
negative** in charge, then eliminate every option with the wrong sign. That alone can take
five options down to two.

This is why vocabulary work should include a rough sense of a word's charge, not only its
definition. See [Learning Words That Pay](./Learning%20Words%20That%20Pay.md).

## What this looks like in practice

> Reviewers dismissed the biography as largely ______, contending that the author had
> fabricated entire conversations.

The clue is everything after the comma: the author invented material. The blank has to
mean roughly "made up". Now the options can be read safely — and "controversial", which
fits the grammar perfectly and would have been tempting cold, is visibly wrong, because it
is not what the sentence said the problem was.

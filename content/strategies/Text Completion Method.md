## The format

One to three blanks in a short passage of dense academic prose.

- **One blank** — five options.
- **Two or three blanks** — three options each.

**There is no partial credit.** A three-blank question with two right is worth exactly the
same as one with none right. That single fact drives the whole method.

## The method

1. **Read the entire passage first.** Every sentence, including the ones without blanks.
   On multi-blank questions the clue for one blank very often lives in a different
   sentence.
2. **Find the blank you are most sure about.** It is rarely the first one. Order of
   appearance has nothing to do with order of difficulty.
3. **Solve that one** using [Finding The Clue Word](./Finding%20The%20Clue%20Word.md).
4. **Use it to constrain the rest.** A settled blank usually fixes the direction of the
   others, because the blanks are written to interact.
5. **Read the completed sentence back in full.** If it does not sound like something a
   careful writer would publish, one of your choices is wrong.

## Why "easiest first" matters so much

With three blanks and three options each, blind guessing gives you a 1 in 27 chance.
Solving one blank confidently makes it 1 in 9. Solving two makes it 1 in 3. Because
scoring is all-or-nothing, each blank you nail multiplies your odds on the whole question
rather than adding to them.

## The traps particular to this format

- **The word that fits the grammar but contradicts the logic.** Always present. This is
  why you commit to your own word before reading the options.
- **The blank that seems to allow two answers.** It does not — you have missed a
  constraint somewhere in a sentence you skimmed. Go back and read the part without
  blanks.
- **Vocabulary you half-know.** A word you have seen but cannot define is more dangerous
  than one you have never met, because familiarity feels like fit.
- **Sentences designed to reverse late.** A "yet" in the final clause can invert the sense
  of everything preceding it. Read to the full stop before deciding anything.

## Pacing

Aim for under 60 seconds on one blank, and under 90 on two or three. Text Completion is
where you bank the time that Reading Comprehension will spend. If you are routinely over
two minutes here, the problem is usually vocabulary rather than method — see
[Learning Words That Pay](./Learning%20Words%20That%20Pay.md).

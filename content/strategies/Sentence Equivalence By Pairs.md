## The format

One sentence, one blank, six options, **exactly two correct**. No partial credit.

The two correct words must satisfy **both** conditions:

1. Each fits the sentence.
2. Together they produce sentences that mean substantially the same thing.

Nearly every wrong answer comes from honouring one condition and forgetting the other.

## The structural insight

Because the answer must be a pair, **any word without a near-synonym among the other five
options cannot be correct.** It could fit the sentence perfectly and it is still
impossible, because there would be nothing to pair it with.

This is a genuine shortcut, and it is unique to this format.

## The method

1. Read the sentence and commit to your own word first, exactly as in
   [Finding The Clue Word](./Finding%20The%20Clue%20Word.md).
2. **Group the six options into pairs by meaning**, before judging fit. You will typically
   find two or three pairs and one or two lonely words.
3. **Cross out every unpaired word immediately.** They are impossible.
4. Test the surviving pairs against the sentence. Usually only one pair fits.

## The trap the format is built around

The test almost always includes **a second synonym pair that does not fit the sentence.**
That pair exists solely to catch people who noticed the pairing rule but stopped checking
meaning.

So the two rules are not interchangeable, and neither alone is sufficient:

- Fits the sentence, but has no partner → impossible.
- Has a partner, but the pair does not fit the sentence → impossible.

You need both, every time.

## Worked shape

> Reviewers dismissed the biography as largely ______, contending that the author had
> fabricated entire conversations.
>
> apocryphal · spurious · controversial · contentious · meticulous · definitive

Two pairs are visible: *apocryphal/spurious* (not genuine) and *controversial/contentious*
(disputed). *Meticulous* and *definitive* are unpaired, so they are gone immediately.

The clue says the author invented material — that is about authenticity, not about dispute.
So the answer is apocryphal and spurious, and the controversial/contentious pair is the
engineered trap: it fits the grammar, it satisfies the pairing rule, and it does not
match what the sentence actually said the problem was.

## The most common self-inflicted error

Finding one word you are certain of and then hunting for a second that "also works". That
inverts the method. The pair is the unit of answer here — think in pairs from the start,
not in single words you later try to match up.
